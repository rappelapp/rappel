Three ways to make the inside of a deck nicer

Max, the deck page works but reads like a spreadsheet. Below are three propositions drawn as GNOME windows, each at desktop size and at phone size (360 px). They use only libadwaita's own widgets and theme colours, so they follow light and dark mode and the user's accent colour. Use the switch to see them in dark mode.

The cards are your SIC 3101 deck. The backs, due dates and counts are invented for the picture.

  • The same tags on every rowFour pills repeated 17 times. They say nothing, since every card has them.
  • No overviewThe page does not tell you what is due, new or being learnt, only the header's one line.
  • Raw mathsA title like Mesure \sigma-finie shows the LaTeX source instead of the formula.
  • Empty sidesOn a big screen the list is a thin column in a wide dark area.
Windows scroll inside, like the app. On a phone, swipe the picture sideways to see the second window.

A · Tidy list

Small change

Same page, cleaned up. The tags that every card shares are said once at the top. A row shows the front, the back under it, and only the tags that make this card different. The list becomes a rounded “boxed list”, as in Settings and most GNOME apps, and the maths is drawn.

Desktop, 900 px

Phone, 360 px

Good

  • Quiet and familiar; nothing new to learn.
  • The engineering is mostly a few rules about which tags to show.

Less good

  • Still no overview of what is due.
  • Still a narrow column on a wide screen.

B · Overview, then cards by state

Recommended

Everything from A, plus a summary at the top (due today, new, learning, how much you remember) and the cards grouped the way you think about them: what is due today, what you are learning, what is new, what comes later. Suspended cards fold away at the bottom. A search field filters the deck. This is close to how GNOME's Settings, Fragments and Health group their content.

Desktop, 900 px

Phone, 360 px

Good

  • Answers “what should I do now?” at a glance.
  • Each row can say when the card is next due.
  • Works the same on a phone; the summary turns into a 2 × 2 square.

Less good

  • More work: the list is split in groups, which must stay fast with thousands of cards.
  • Order changes as you study, since cards move between groups.

C · Card tiles

Largest change

Like Mochi, or a photo grid: each card is a small tile with its front drawn large and its back under it. A tile shows its state as a chip. A switch in the top strip goes back to a list, and a narrow window always gets the list. It uses the full width of a big screen.

Desktop, 900 px

Phone, 360 px (falls back to the list)

Good

  • The prettiest; feels like a pack of real cards.
  • Fills a wide window.

Less good

  • Shows about 12 cards per screen instead of 10 to 20; slower to scan long decks.
  • Long fronts, images and big formulas need a careful tile design.
  • Two layouts to build and test.

My recommendation: B

B fixes all four problems except the empty sides on a very wide window, which GNOME apps usually accept for lists (Settings and Fragments do the same). It keeps the page a list, so it stays fast and keyboard-friendly, and selection mode, the row menus and the header stay exactly as they are.

A is a good first step if you want something quick: it is a subset of B, so nothing is thrown away. C is the most striking, but I would keep it as an optional “grid view” for later.

In all three, the maths in a title needs the formula renderer inside the list, which the Lead engineer will have to check for speed.
