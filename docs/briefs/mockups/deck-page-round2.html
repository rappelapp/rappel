The deck page, second try

Max, you were right: with fronts only, A turns into a tall stack of identical grey bars, and on a wide window it sits in a thin column. These three propositions keep the one thing you liked, the shared tags said once at the top, and fix the rest in different ways. They use your real SIC 3101 fronts, with no backs. The due dates are invented.

  • Every row looks the sameOne line of text per row, 21 times, with nothing to rest the eye on.
  • Heavy grey slabThe boxed list fills a 600 px column with card colour, top to bottom.
  • Nothing to say about a cardWithout a back, the row has no second line, so it holds no information beyond the name.
Windows scroll inside, like the app. On a phone, swipe sideways to see the second window.

D · Rows that say when

Closest to what is built

Each row gets a second line in every case: the back when the card has one, otherwise when the card comes up next (“Due today”, “In 3 days”, “New”). A small dot at the start shows the state at a glance: filled blue for due, amber for learning, a green ring for new, grey for later. The extra tags stay on the right. The rows look like real list items again rather than bars.

Desktop, 900 px

Phone, 360 px

Good

  • Every row tells you something useful.
  • A small change from the page you have, so it is quick to build.

Less good

  • Still a long column on a wide window.
  • A dot of colour needs its word too (the subtitle gives it), for colour-blind users.

E · Grouped by tag, no boxes

The calmest

The shared tags at the top, and the extra tags go one step further: cards that carry the same extra tags are listed together under those tags, so no row shows a pill at all. The list sits on the page itself with thin separators, as in Files or Contacts, with no grey box behind it. The due state is a short word on the right.

Desktop, 900 px

Phone, 360 px

Good

  • No pills on rows at all: very quiet.
  • The groups break the long list into readable parts.

Less good

  • Cards are no longer in the order you added them; they follow their tags.
  • A deck where every card has different tags gets many small groups (past a few, it falls back to plain rows).

F · Small cards

Uses the whole window

Short fronts like yours suit small tiles: each card is a little card, the front in bold, its state and extra tags at the bottom. The grid fills a wide window, so you see the whole deck at once, and a phone gets two columns. This is the C idea from round 1, made compact for front-only cards.

Desktop, 900 px

Phone, 360 px

Good

  • Feels like a pack of cards; the prettiest.
  • No empty sides: about 25 cards fit on a laptop screen instead of 15.

Less good

  • Long fronts are cut after three lines.
  • The most work: a grid with keyboard focus, selection mode and fast scrolling for big decks.

My recommendation: E, or D if you want the smallest step

E is the calmest: no boxes, no pills on rows, and groups that give the list a shape. It fits a deck like yours, where a few cards share extra tags. D is the safe choice: it keeps the built page and simply gives every row a useful second line. F is the prettiest but also the most work.

Parts can be mixed, for example E's flat list with D's “when” line.
